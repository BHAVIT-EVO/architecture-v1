### Pattern the data actually shows
- **Almost every winner started narrow, concrete, and category-of-one-adjacent, not grand.** "A screen recorder" (Loom), "a mesh VPN deployed in minutes" (Tailscale), "a command-line inspired native app to save developers time" (Raycast), "an instant-message-based team communication tool" (Slack). The expansive category language ("Zero Trust Networking," "all-in-one workspace," "your home on the internet") came **later**, after the narrow product worked.
- **Arc is the lone example that led with category-creation language** ("a brand new category of software") — and Arc is the one that was put into maintenance mode and folded into Dia/Atlassian. That is a cautionary data point, not a success template.
- Slack's "We Don't Sell Saddles Here" is the report's cleanest example of selling the *job* ("ride a horse better") rather than the *tool* ("saddles").

## 3. The case AGAINST category creation (report's analytical core)

The report argues most companies should *not* try to create a category:

> "most startups confuse product differentiation with category creation"

> "inventing language is cheap; creating a market is not."

**The three jobs category creation actually requires** (all three, or it fails):
1. Teach the market that the problem exists at all.
2. Convince buyers the problem deserves budget.
3. Establish the comparison framework buyers will use to evaluate you.

**The decision test (usable verbatim on a slide):**
> "If the buyer already understands the problem and can name several existing solutions, you are probably entering a category. If the first sales conversation must establish why the problem matters at all, you are attempting category creation."

Supporting academic reference: an **SSRN paper [39]** distinguishing *category creation* from *category emergence* (categories that form around you vs. ones you must manufacture).

**Implication for Evo:** if buyers already know "I lose my work context when I switch projects" and can name Rewind, tab managers, etc., Evo is *entering* a category (cheaper, faster) — not creating one. Leading with "a brand new category of software" is the Arc move, and Arc is the failure in this dataset.

## 4. Report Quality Problems

The honesty is the headline; the incompleteness and secondary-sourcing are the costs.

### 1. No Wayback URLs — the task's core artifact is missing
The assignment required archived first-capture URLs. The report provides none and says it would not fabricate them. Honest, but the deliverable's primary artifact is absent. Every "earliest" quote below is therefore **unverified against a timestamped archive.**

### 2. "Earliest" wording is repeatedly sourced to secondary summaries
- Figma's earliest line ("creatively express themselves online") is cited to **Wikipedia [1]**, not a 2012 archive.
- Slack's "We Don't Sell Saddles Here" memo is quoted from **secondary republications**, not the primary 2013 posting.
- Other verifications lean on `nira.com`, `taskade.com`, `hibtrecaps.com`, `marketingexamples.com`. These are decent secondary sources, but "earliest public words" is a claim that specifically requires primary/archived text, and the report substitutes summaries.

### 3. Cursor citation mismatch
The body says Cursor's "a code editor built for programming with AI" comes from **Hacker News**, but the citation attached is **[21] a16z**. Source and claim disagree. Do not present Cursor's earliest wording as verified until the actual first posting is found.

### 4. Only ~half the requested companies
9–10 of 20 verified; Airtable, Retool, Vercel, Stripe, Roam, Granola, Rewind, Descript, Superhuman, Linear explicitly declined. The positioning slide has half the comparative base it was scoped for.

### 5. Citation padding (worst of the three reports)
~66 of 105 sources (~63%) are never cited in the body. Nearly two-thirds of the source list is decorative.

### What IS available and legitimate (for re-verification)
Strong primaries the report either used lightly or gestured at: `figma.com/blog`, `raycast.com/blog`, `tailscale.com/blog`, `businesswire.com`, `news.ycombinator.com` (the Show HN posts for Raycast and Tailscale are real and datable), and the SSRN paper [39]. The correct earliest-wording quotes are almost all recoverable from these plus the Wayback Machine — the report just didn't finish that pass.

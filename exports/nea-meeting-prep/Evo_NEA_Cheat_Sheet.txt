THE PAPER HE CO-WROTE
03 DSPy
What it is, plainly. Before DSPy, an LLM pipeline was a pile of prompt strings someone tuned by hand until it
worked. DSPy says: stop writing prompts, write a program.
Declare each step — “takes a question, returns an answer” — wire them together, and let a compiler search for
the instructions and worked examples that maximise a metric you defined. The prompt stops being
something you hand-craft and becomes a parameter the compiler finds.
WHAT TO KNOW
The paper
DSPy: Compiling Declarative Language Model Calls into State-of-the-Art Pipelines. ICLR 2024, spotlight. First
author Omar Khattab; senior authors include Matei Zaharia and Christopher Potts. Thirteen authors;
Thomas T. Joshi is the ninth.
The claim
Within minutes of compiling, it beats out-of-the-box few-shot prompting and expert-written demonstrations on
GPT-3.5 and Llama2-13b. A compiled 770M T5 becomes competitive with large proprietary models driven by
hand-written prompt chains.
NEA’s line
They call it “the most popular open source software to come out of Stanford AI,” used by Meta and Microsoft.
His bio says co-author. Not “the creator.”
The bridge — the best thing you can say to this man
DSPy’s real idea is not the compiler. It is that you do not hand-tune the system; you declare what must
be true and let the build be measured against it. Your gate is the same move, applied to something
deterministic.
SAY THIS
“The thing I took from DSPy isn’t the library — there’s no language model in my restore path. It’s the
discipline. You don’t hand-tune the grouping; you declare the properties that must hold and let the build fail
when they don’t. Six assertions: corpus is real, labels are real, something is reconstructed, no two works
fuse, no leisure resource ever anchors a thread, same input gives the same output twice. Same idea,
deterministic system.”
WHY IT WORKS
True, specific, checkable in his head, and it connects his own published work to yours without flattering
him. Associates get flattered constantly. Almost nobody says “here is how your paper changed how I
build the test that fails me.”
Three traps
TRAP
Don’t say “you wrote DSPy,” and don’t claim you use it. He is one of thirteen authors; NEA’s own
bio says co-author, which is correct and impressive — “creator” is wrong and he will correct you. And
there is no LM in your loop, so if you say “we’re doing DSPy for work grouping,” his next question is
“which teleprompter?” and you have nothing.
TRAP
Don’t say “we do RL.” You don’t. Say deterministic core plus a planned per-user fit. Different
words, and he will know within one question.

ONE LINE EACH
04 Twelve questions
Left column is what he says. Right column is the answer — short on purpose. If you find yourself extending an
answer past two sentences, stop and ask him a question instead.
IF HE ASKS
ANSWER
“Did
 you
 read
 the
 neolab
piece?”
“Twice. The three-layer bit is the reason I built the gate the way I did.”
“What did you take from it?”
“Corpus, loop, verifier. I have two of the three and I’d rather tell you which one is
missing.”
“Which one is missing?”
“The loop. My core is deterministic. The learning I’d add is a small per-user fit on
declarations — that’s not an RL loop and I’m not going to call it one.”
“Then you’re not a neolab.”
“No. I’m a product company in a neolab-shaped thesis, at pre-seed. Calling myself
a neolab at $1.5M would be silly.”
“Where’s your verifier?”
“A corpus of 164 labels across six real works from one person’s two days, and six
assertions that fail the build. If two works ever fuse, exit code 1.”
“Isn’t that just a test suite?”
“A test suite checks that the code runs. This checks that the boundary is still
correct — it’s six properties, not six behaviours.”
“How big is the corpus really?”
“Ten thousand events, two days, one person, six works. It’s small. I know exactly
how small.”
“Who owns the corpus?”
“The user. It never leaves the machine — there’s no HTTP client in the build.
That’s the whole reason the layer has to be neutral.”
“What’s DSPy, in one line?”
“Declare the steps, define the metric, let a compiler find the prompt. The metric is
the program.”
“Do you use it?”
“No — no language model in the loop. I took the metric discipline, not the library.”
“What would you compile if you
could?”
“The boundary, not the safety. Learned layer on top, deterministic core
underneath, and the core is the part that can’t be argued with.”
“Why won’t OpenAI build this?”
“They did — Computer History, August. It’s ungrouped, because their unit is the
conversation. What they can’t add is neutrality: a layer that lets Claude be safe on
your desktop is against their interest.”
The answer that wins the room
If he gives you one sentence to defend, make it this one — it is the only claim you can make that nobody else in
the category can.
“Every layer in that piece depends on someone owning the environment. My user owns it,
not me. That’s why the corpus can be neutral, and neutrality is the only thing that makes a
jurisdiction layer worth anything.”

07  The business model he will test to
destruction
At pre-seed nobody expects a proven model. They expect a model that is coherent — where the 
pricing, the product, the distribution and the retention story all point the same way. Here is the 
coherence argument, and the three places it is weakest. 
07.1  The coherence argument, in one paragraph
SAY THIS
The product has three properties that make it a business rather than a feature. It is local , so the free 
tier costs us almost nothing to serve — free is an acquisition strategy, not a subsidy. It compounds , 
because classification improves with tenure, so the switching cost grows with time rather than 
shrinking. And it expands naturally : the same body of work follows you to a second device, which is 
exactly where the paid layer sits. Local, compounding, cross-device — that's the whole model.

08  The fifteen commercial questions
These are the questions that will come to you. Answer each in under 45 seconds, with one number, 
then stop. The silence after a short answer is where he decides you are credible. 
CQ1.  "How big is the market?"
SAY THIS
Bottom-up: 6.1 million US computer and mathematical professionals. If we activate 1.6% in year one 
that's 100,000 users; 9% paying at $108 is about $970K ARR. The adjacent ring is 70.7 million 
management and professional occupations, and the ceiling is roughly a billion knowledge workers 
worldwide. 
WARNING
Do not open with the billion. 
CQ2.  "Who is the customer, exactly?"
SAY THIS
A developer, designer or founder who works on three or more projects at once, across more than five 
apps, and loses their place at least once a day. Not 'knowledge workers'. That person, described 
precisely. 
WARNING
The narrower the description, the more credible the market. 
CQ3.  "Why would someone pay $9 a month for this?"
SAY THIS
Because the alternative is ten minutes a day of reconstruction. At a $60/hour blended rate, ten 
minutes a day is $2,500 a year. Nine dollars a month is a rounding error against that — but only if we 
actually save the ten minutes, which is what the retention number proves. 
WARNING
Never argue from 'it's cheap'. Argue from the value of the time. 
CQ4.  "What's the CAC?"
SAY THIS
Unproven. The plan is organic: the demo clip, Show HN, Product Hunt, creator videos and a warm list of 
300. If the clip doesn't travel, we'll know in six weeks and the model changes. 
WARNING
Do not invent a CAC number. 'Unproven' is a legitimate pre-seed answer. 
CQ5.  "What's the churn assumption?"
SAY THIS
I don't have one I'd defend. The design assumption is that churn falls with tenure because the 
product's value compounds. The first 1,000 users will tell us whether that's true. 
WARNING
An invented churn number is worse than none.

23  Three objections, scripted in full
These are the three commercial objections that most often end a pre-seed memo. Each script is 
written to be spoken — roughly 60–90 seconds — with the structure: concede the true part, 
reframe the frame, give the number, name the test. Do not memorise the words; memorise the 
four moves. 
Objection 1 — "This is a feature, not a company."
The move: concede that the observation layer is a feature. Then show that the thing you own is not the 
observation. 
SAY THIS
I agree with the premise, and it's the reason I don't pitch this as a memory product. Watching what 
happens on a screen is a feature — it's a weekend project now, there are open-source versions, and 
OpenAI ships it for free inside ChatGPT. If that were the company, you'd be right to pass. What we own 
is two things that aren't features: the classification that decides which body of work an activity belongs 
to, and the execution layer that reopens the machine in the right state. Both of those require a local 
daemon, a per-app capability model, and a corpus that accumulates per user. That's not a feature; 
that's a system. And the test is simple — if it were a feature, a competitor could ship it in a quarter. 
Nobody has, in a year and a half. 
WARNING
The trap is getting louder about the feature claim. Concede it fully in the first sentence, then move the 
frame to what is not a feature. If you argue that observation is hard, you lose — because it isn't any 
more. 
Objection 2 — "The market is a niche of Mac power users."
The move: accept the shape of the beachhead, then show the monetisation expands before the audience 
does. 
SAY THIS
The beachhead is narrow — I'd rather have a narrow beachhead than a broad maybe. But two things 
expand, and they expand in a specific order. First the product: the same Mac app becomes a paid 
cross-device product at month five, so revenue per user triples before the audience changes at all. 
Second the platform: Windows doubles the addressable base and the iPhone capture surface makes 
the corpus continuous, which is the thing that makes the paid layer feel necessary. The audience that 
matters in year one is 6.1 million US computer and mathematical professionals, and we need 1.6% of 
it. That's not a niche argument — that's a penetration argument. 
GOOD TO KNOW
The phrase "revenue per user triples before the audience changes" is the strongest sentence in this 
book. It answers the market-size objection with the pricing model rather than with a TAM. 
Objection 3 — "Prosumer subscriptions never become
venture-scale."
The move: agree with the base rate, then show the two structural differences that make Evo not a 
typical prosumer subscription.

The base rate says you're right: most prosumer subscriptions don't get there, because they charge five

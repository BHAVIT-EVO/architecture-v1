NO JARGON
01 How Evo works, in plain English
The one sentence
SAY THIS
Evo watches how you work, groups it into your separate projects, and puts your desk back exactly
as it was when you come back.
That is the whole product. Everything else in this book is detail underneath those three clauses.
The desk
Picture your computer as a desk. All day you pile things on it — documents, browser tabs, files,
folders, terminal windows. You're working on four different things at once and it's a mess, but it's
your mess and you know where everything is.
Then the phone rings. You take a forty-minute call. Or you go to lunch. Or you sleep.
You come back and you've lost it. Not the files — they're all still there. You've lost where you were
. So you spend ten minutes clicking tabs, opening folders, trying to remember which of the forty
open windows was the one that mattered.
Evo is the assistant who was sitting next to you the whole time. When you come back, everything
else is cleared away and the right pile is in front of you — the document on top, the three tabs you
needed, the folder open underneath. You don't search, you don't remember. You just keep going.
How it knows
Three mechanisms, in order. This is the part worth internalising, because it is the answer to almost
every technical question he can ask.
It watches
A small program runs in the background on your Mac. It notices which window you're looking at,
what file you saved, what page you opened, what you typed into, when you committed code. It does
this all day, quietly.
It never takes screenshots. It isn't recording your screen. It sees what you're working in and
when — not what's inside it. That distinction is the entire privacy argument, and it is also the
reason the product can run for weeks without eating your battery.
It groups
Everything you do gets chopped into chunks: a stretch of time where you stayed on one thing. Type
in a file, save it, switch to the browser, come back, save again — that's one chunk of one job.
The cutting is done by content, not by the clock. If you glance at a notification for ninety seconds
and come back, that's not two chunks — it's one chunk with an interruption in it. Only a gap long
enough to be a real departure (twenty minutes of silence, by default) ends the chunk.

It decides what belongs to what
This is the actual invention, and it is one idea:
SAY THIS
It doesn't look at what you have open. It looks at what you produced.
Things you only looked at are context. Things you typed in, saved, downloaded, or committed to are
identity. That single rule is why a browser with fifteen tabs across three different projects doesn't
confuse it — the tabs you didn't work in don't get a vote.
And if something genuinely belongs to two projects, it refuses to guess. It shows you both and
lets you pick. It would rather be unsure than be confidently wrong.
It remembers
When you correct it — drag a window into the right project, merge two, split one — that correction
is permanent and outranks anything it figured out on its own. You teach it once.
What you do
You say: "Hey Evo, continue the pitch deck."
It figures out which project you mean, then reopens what that project needs — the document, the
tabs you had open alongside it, the folder. Five things at most. Not everything you ever touched,
just enough to pick up where you left off.
No searching. No file browser. No remembering. You were interrupted; now you're back.
Why it's different from everything else
Every other tool answers "where is that thing?" — Spotlight, Recall, Rewind, ChatGPT's memory,
your browser history. They are all search. They all assume you know what you're looking for and
just can't find it.
Evo answers a different question: "where was I?"
That is not a search problem. It is a state problem — what was open, in what arrangement, on this
machine. Nothing on the web can answer it, because the answer isn't on the web. It's on your
laptop, in the mess you left behind.
SAY THIS
"Search assumes you know what you lost. Evo knows where you were."
WHY THIS WORKS
That line is worth memorising. It is the whole positioning in twelve words, it is true, and it
survives any follow-up because it is a statement about the shape of the problem rather than a
claim about your technology.

Why stage five is the interesting one
Anybody can launch an app. The hard part is that restoring is not opening . If the document is

This is the part almost no consumer product does, and it is the thing to point at when he asks how
you know it works.

this." The person trusts the second one and corrects it. The first one teaches them the product lies,
and they stop using it within a week.

What the four seconds are not
CAREFUL
It is not a chat. There is no model generating text, no prompt, no response. The voice path is a
command channel, not a conversation. If he asks "what model do you use for the assistant," the
honest answer is: "There isn't one in the loop. There's a wake-word model, a transcription model
and an embedding model, and then deterministic code. I deliberately didn't put a language model
in the path — latency, battery, and the fact that it would make the product less predictable, not
more."
The second thing it is not: it is not a search box. Search asks you to remember what you are
looking for. The whole premise is that you cannot — that the work is real and the label for it is not
in your head at 9am. If you ever describe Evo as “search over your stuff”, you have explained it as a
feature of Spotlight and lost the argument before you started.
The third: it is not a productivity score. Nothing in the build rates you, grades your day, or tells
you that you were 62% focused. That entire genre of product is a different thesis — it assumes the
problem is discipline. Yours assumes the problem is continuity. Say the difference out loud, because
it is one of the few places where you can cleanly separate yourself from a crowded shelf.
The sentence that survives interruption
There are four seconds of speech above, and you will rarely get to deliver them in order. An
associate will interrupt at second two with a question about whether this is just window
management. So what you actually need is one sentence that carries the whole idea and survives
being cut off:
The work already happened. The product reopens it.
Everything else in this section is elaboration. If you get cut off, come back to that sentence, then
answer the interruption, then say: “and the reason that’s hard is the two works problem” — which
is §07.

What the gate actually asserts
Six checks, in order. Learn these; they are the substance behind the word "evaluation."
1. The corpus is real. More than 100 events, and Typed events must be present. That second one
looks pedantic and isn't: without input evidence, you cannot tell whether a resource was worked in
or merely looked at, so placement claims become untestable. The test refuses to run on a corpus
that can't disambiguate.
2. The labels are real. More than 20 hand labels, so a stub can't pass.
3. Something is reconstructed. At least one body of work must be minted from a real day. An
engine that outputs nothing cannot fail a safety test, so this closes that loophole.
4. No fusion. No thread may contain two different labelled works. This is the expensive failure — a
client's material appearing in another client's room.
5. No leisure anchor. A resource labelled leisure may never become the anchor of a body of work.
Leisure can ride along as context; it cannot found a work.
6. Determinism. The same corpus, replayed, must produce identical thread identities. If replay is
not deterministic, every other guarantee is unfalsifiable.

THE ANSWER YOU CANNOT BLUFF
04 Does it learn?
You already know the engine is dumb in this respect. Here is the part that matters: this is the
worst possible investor to bluff on it. Thomas co-authored DSPy. His published position is that
the hard half of AI is problem definition and owning evaluation. If you say "it learns," the next three
questions are what's the update rule, what's the label, where's the loss — and there are no answers
in the repository.
What is true today
Anchors do not decay. Once you produce in a resource, it stays identity. What you made doesn't
stop being what you made.
Companions do decay — a three-day half-life. Context drifts; identity doesn't.
Declarations are permanent and outrank inference. SameWork , SeparateWork , NotMine ,
Classified , WorkDismissed , MemberState . The code calls them the supreme court of attribution.
A merge requires a declaration. There is no function reachable from inference that can merge
two threads. The merge code path takes a declaration or it does not run.
Episodes are content-tested. Resumption is decided by whether you touched the same material,
not by whether enough time passed.
So the picture of your work genuinely gets richer with tenure. What it does not do is change its
own weights.
The sentence to use
SAY THIS
"Today it compounds memory, not parameters. Every correction is permanent and outranks
anything the engine infers. What it doesn't do yet is fit its own weights — that's the next layer."
Then, if he pushes:
SAY THIS
"Correct. It accumulates evidence today and adapts next. I'd rather show you the boundary than
have you find it in diligence."
WHY THIS WORKS
That answer is worth more than any claim, because it tells him you know which half of your
system is which. A founder who can name the exact boundary of his own capability is treated as
an operator; one who rounds up is treated as a salesperson.

Why a tiny model is the right call, not a compromise
He may wonder why you would not just run a small language model over the log. The answer is
practical and confident:
The decision is narrow. "Does this belong with that" is a binary call on structured features.
Logistic regression is close to optimal for that shape of problem and fully inspectable.
It is auditable. You can print the weights and explain any single decision. You cannot do that with
a transformer.
It costs nothing. Microseconds, no battery, no thermal budget.
It fails honestly. A linear model near its decision boundary produces a genuine near-tie — which
is exactly the condition where the product already forks and asks.

Cold start and the moat
Cold start: population priors ship inside the binary; the per-user model starts at the prior and
moves as evidence arrives. Day one isn't empty; day thirty is better.
Moat, honestly stated: not the model. Anyone can copy a logistic regression. The moat is the
per-user evidence graph plus the corrections — six months of this belonged with that , which
cannot be copied, only re-earned. Switching cost is tenure, not features. That is the true version of
the compounding claim.
The test that would kill the compounding story
If he is doing his job, he is asking himself: how would I know if this is wrong? Give him the answer,
because a founder who names the falsifying test is unmistakably serious.
SAY THIS
"The number that decides whether any of this is real is how often a user corrects the engine. If
people correct it a few times in the first week and then stop, the compounding story is dead — it
means either it got good enough that corrections don't happen, or people stopped caring. If they
keep correcting it for months, the boundary is genuinely hard and genuinely accumulating, and
there's a model to fit."
Then the honest caveat:
SAY THIS
"I don't have that number yet, because nobody is on the current build. It's the first thing I'd
instrument after the round, and it's cheap — it's a count of a thing the app already records."
WHY THIS WORKS
This is a much stronger position than it sounds. Most founders claim a compounding advantage
and cannot describe what evidence would disprove it. You have just described the experiment,
named the metric, said what result would kill the thesis, and pointed out that the data collection
costs nothing. That is research discipline, which is the thing he actually respects.

THE PRIVACY QUESTION
05 Where does it learn if nothing leaves
the device?
This is the sharpest version of the challenge, and it is fair: if the data never leaves, and there is no
cloud, how can the product improve? The answer has three parts.
One: the learning is per-user, so it belongs on the device
The model that needs to improve is not a general model. It is your model of your work. There is
exactly one user per model. Fitting it anywhere other than your machine would be slower, less

The distinction, concretely
An agent with memory knows you worked on three things this week.

THE MOST USEFUL SKILL IN THE ROOM
17 When you don't know
He can ask anything and you cannot know everything. What he is testing is not your knowledge — it
is whether you know the edges of your own knowledge , because that predicts whether you will
be honest with him for the next five years.
The four beats
1. Acknowledge. "I don't know — that's a real gap." No flinch, no filler.
2. Nearest true thing. "What I do know is ___."
3. How you'd find out, and when. "The way I'd answer it is ___; I can have that to you by Tuesday."
4. Turn it. "How would you test it?" or "What would you need to see?"
SAY THIS
"I don't know. That's a gap, not a hedge. What I do know is the shape of the answer — it's a
storage-growth question and I'd measure it on my own machine over a week rather than guess. I
can send you the number by Wednesday. How would you sanity-check it?"
For design questions — show the axes
If it is not a fact you're missing but a decision you haven't made, do not fake an answer. Show the
trade-off:
SAY THIS

the thing people ask for, and it's also the first time anything leaves the machine. My instinct is
encrypted sync where the key never touches the server, but I haven't pressure-tested it. How
have you seen local-first companies handle that?"

Never argue with a question. If he says "this looks like a feature," do not say "no it isn't." Say
"that's the risk I think about most" and then give him the counter-argument.
Never blame the market, the timing, or other investors. Fastest way to look junior.
The recovery, in one paragraph
When an answer goes badly — and one will — do not try to rescue it in the same breath. The
recovery has three beats and takes about fifteen seconds:
1. Name it. “That was a bad answer.” Said plainly, without a laugh, it buys you more credibility than
a good answer would have.
2. Say what the good answer was. One sentence. The version you would have given if you had
thought for another ten seconds.
3. Move. Ask him a question, or return to the thread. Do not apologise twice and do not explain why
you were nervous.
The reason this works is that he is not scoring your performance. He is trying to find out whether
you are the kind of person who can be told something is wrong in month four and act on it. A
founder who names a bad answer cleanly has just shown him that, in fifteen seconds, with no
preparation. That is worth more than a polished answer to a question he has asked forty times.
What not to do in that moment: do not fill the silence with a longer version of the bad answer.
Volume reads as panic, and panic is the one thing in the room that is actually contagious.

IF YOU KEEP NOTHING ELSE
18 The five sentences
These are the five that carry the most weight per word. Learn them as ideas rather than as lines,
so they survive being paraphrased under pressure.
1. "It compounds memory, not parameters — and the learned layer is next, fitted on-device
from your own corrections."
2. "The corpus is an answer key, not an input; it's six works from one day, and I know
exactly how small that is."
3. "Failure is designed to be cheap: fork instead of guess, abstain on shared resources,
never merge without a declaration."
4. "Time-to-resume is computable from a log that never leaves the machine, and I'm
instrumenting it now rather than quoting it."
5. "Marginal cost is near zero because there is no inference bill — the intelligence runs on
the user's laptop."
One last thing, and it is the thing to remember when your hands are shaking at 11:29pm.
You got this meeting because you wrote one specific email to one specific person about
something she had actually written. That is the same skill this call requires: say the true,
specific thing to the person in front of you, and then stop talking. You already know how
to do it.
Everything else in this book is preparation for the moment when you have to do that for thirty

3.2  How a deal moves
NEA's process is unusually well documented for a firm its size. Fortune described the core of it in 2011 
and it still describes the shape today: 
1. A small sector team owns the deal. Someone with domain knowledge — here, Thomas — runs 
the evaluation and writes the case. 
2. The partnership meets every Monday. Deals are brought to the full partnership, not decided by 
one partner. 
3. Two votes. In the first, the sector partners vote YE (yes, enthusiastic), YS (yes, with 
reservations) or NO . A "YS" deal gets conditions attached. 
4. Then diligence and a second vote before money moves. 
5. Timeline: typically four to eight weeks from first serious interest to close; two to three weeks 
when a founder arrives pre-referred and the case is uncontested. Nothing about a pre-seed at 
this size will be fast. 
EVIDENCE
Fortune, "NEA: the VC world's best-kept secret", 7 Nov 2011 — the Monday partnership meeting, the 
YE/YS/NO vote, and the two-vote structure. The four-to-eight-week window comes from 
investor-database summaries of NEA, not from NEA itself; treat it as an order of magnitude, not a 
promise.

04  The five questions he is actually asking
Thomas published his own evaluation framework before he ever saw your deck. Every question 
below is a translation of it. If you answer these five well, the standard VC questions answer 
themselves. 
Q1 — "Is this a capability or a utility?"
His words: we have a working recipe for capability; the second half is about defining the right problems 
so products create real economic value. 
What he is testing: does Evo change an outcome that someone would pay for, or is it a clever demo of 
OS APIs? He has watched a hundred "AI memory" demos. He is not impressed by capture. He is 
impressed by a measurable change in what happens next. 
SAY THIS
The capability is everywhere — OpenAI shipped Computer History in August, Microsoft shipped Recall, 
there are open-source capture tools that are a weekend project. None of them change what happens 
when you come back to work. Evo's utility is one number: how long it takes you to be working again 
after an interruption. Today that's 25 minutes. In our harness it's under 30 seconds, and I can show 
you where it fails. 
WARNING
Do not describe the product as "an AI that remembers your work." That is the capability everyone has. 
Describe it as a restoration action with a measured outcome. 
Q2 — "Is this the right problem, or a problem you find interesting?"
What he is testing: whether you chose this problem from evidence or from personal annoyance. Personal 
annoyance is a legitimate origin — but only if you can show it generalises. 
SAY THIS
I started because I was rebuilding 26 windows every morning. But I didn't trust that. I ran 50+ 
interviews with people who described this exact problem in their own words on Reddit and in person, 
and then 300+ conversations at hackathons and events. What came back was the same sentence in 
different vocabularies: developers lose their terminal and PR state, designers lose the client file and 
the review thread, founders lose the deck and the decision. The pain is not "I can't find things." It's "I 
can't get back into it." 
EVIDENCE
Mark, González & Harris (CHI 2005): 24 information workers, 700+ hours observed. 57% of work 
segments were interrupted; 77% of interrupted work was resumed the same day; when resumed, it 
took 25 min 26 s on average, with 2.26 other tasks done in between. Use this — it is the real number, 
and your deck already cites it correctly. 
Q3 — "How do you know it works? Show me your evaluation."
This is the heart of him. DSPy exists because prompting by vibes does not scale; SWE-Bench-CL exists 
because agents forget. He will not accept "it feels good." He will want to know how you decide whether a 
restoration was correct.

SAY THIS
There are two metrics I care about. First, classification: did the engine put this window in the right body 
of work? We have a labelled corpus and a regression harness — the ground-truth gate runs on every 
commit and it is hard-failing, not advisory. Second, restoration fidelity: when we replay a body of work, 
does the workspace come back — the right app, the right file, the right tab, the right working 
directory? We measure it per work body, and we know exactly which ones fail. 
GOOD TO KNOW
Your repository already has this: a ground-truth regression gate, a shadow gate on real logs, and a test 
suite that the build log records at 57 suites / 1,087 passing. If you have run it recently, quote the 
number from the terminal, not from memory. If you have not run it since the last change, run it before 
Monday and have the output on screen in a second window. 
Q4 — "Is the memory longitudinal, and does it compound?"
His words: living benchmarks, longitudinal memory and agency , outcome-grounded metrics that run 
inside production workflows. 
SAY THIS
The engine learns a user's bodies of work over time. In the alpha, the failure mode we saw was 
cross-contamination — a thesis and a side project merging in week one. By week three the engine had 
separated them, because the evidence accumulated: different repos, different documents, different 
times of day. That's the compounding. A search index doesn't get better with tenure. A classification 
engine does. That is also why the switching cost is time, not features. 
GOOD TO KNOW
This is the single most NEA-shaped argument you have: the product gets better the longer you use it, 
and therefore retention is the moat . It also converts your weakest point — only 26 users — into a 
structural claim about why tenure matters, without pretending you have scale. 
Q5 — "Where is the outcome, not the output?"
What he is testing: can you name a business metric that is not a vanity metric? "Users restored context" 
is output. "Hours returned per week" or "paid conversion after 30 days" is outcome. 
SAY THIS
Two outcomes, and I'll be honest about which one I can measure today. The user outcome is 
time-to-resume. We can measure it locally without sending anything to a server: timestamp of 
interruption, timestamp of restore, timestamp of first meaningful action in the restored app. The 
business outcome is 30-day paid conversion, and I can't measure that yet because Pro doesn't exist 
until month three. What I have is a 26-person cohort and a plan to instrument the next 1,000 users 
from day one.

09  The technical interrogation
This is where you have an advantage most founders do not: he is an engineer who will understand 
your answers, and he will respect an honest limitation more than a confident hand-wave. Do not try 
to sound like a distributed-systems researcher. Sound like the person who built the thing and knows 
where it breaks. 
T1.  "Walk me through the pipeline. What happens between a click
and a restored workspace?"
Five stages, and they're separate crates so each one is testable in isolation. Observation turns OS events 
— app focus, window title, document identity, browser tab, file open — into a normalised event stream. 
Artifact resolves identity: the same document opened twice is one artifact, not two events. Engagement is 
where dwell and interaction become evidence that this artifact mattered. Threads and workspace form 
bodies of work by clustering that evidence. Restoration turns a body of work into a plan — which app, 
which resource, in what order — and execution runs it natively through the OS. The important design 
decision is that identity is separate from membership: the same URL can belong to two different works, 
because the evidence around it differs. 
WARNING
Do not claim the pipeline is finished or that every stage is equally mature. The honest status is that 
observation, ledger and restoration are solid, and classification is the part that still needs the most 
work. Say that. 
T2.  "How do you evaluate classification? What's your ground truth?"
Two layers. A labelled corpus of real work sessions — hand-labelled bodies of work with the evidence that 
belongs to each — and a regression gate that runs on every commit and fails the build if classification 
regresses on that corpus. The second layer is a shadow gate: replay real captured logs through the 
current engine and compare the output to the last accepted output, so I can see drift on data I didn't 
label. The thing I'd want to build next is a per-user evaluation: the user's corrections — merge, split, 
rename — are labelled data by definition, and they're the only ground truth that scales.

for a number, give the pass rate on your corpus and the size of it, and say what it does not cover. 
T3.  "What happens when it gets it wrong?"
It will get it wrong, and the design assumption is that the user corrects it and the correction is durable. 
Merge two works, split one, rename one, exclude a resource — each of those is stored as an explicit 
assertion that outranks inferred evidence. The failure mode I care about is silent wrongness: the engine 
confidently putting a client's work into another client's workspace. That's why the surfacing is 
conservative — better to show two works and let you pick than to merge them and be wrong. 
WARNING
Do not say "it learns automatically and gets better." He will ask how, and "it just does" ends the 
conversation. Describe the mechanism: explicit assertions outrank inferred evidence, and the 
assertions are the training signal. 
T4.  "Restoration is the hard part. What breaks?"
Three classes. Apps with no scripting interface — Electron apps vary wildly, and some provide nothing 
beyond a window. Browser tabs are a per-browser adapter problem: Chrome, Safari, Arc all expose 
different state. And terminal working directories, which are recoverable for some shells and not others. My 
approach is a capability tier per app: full restore, partial restore with a best-effort path, or honest failure 
that tells the user what it could not reopen. I would rather be right about 80% of apps and honest about

T5.  "What's actually running on-device, and what does it cost in

The voice path is local: a wake-word model on ONNX Runtime, Whisper base.en for transcription, and a 
local embedding model for intent matching. No cloud call in the path. The classification engine is not a

make the product worse. The daemon is idle-cheap by design; expensive work is triggered, not

cost. 
T6.  "Why Rust? And why a daemon instead of an app?"
Rust because the core is a long-running local process touching the filesystem, the window server and 
SQLite concurrently, and I wanted memory safety without a garbage collector pause in the middle of a 
restore. A daemon because observation has to outlive the UI: the app can be closed, the engine still needs 
to know what happened. Splitting them also means the engine is testable without a GUI, which is why the 
test suite can run in CI. 
WARNING
The landmine is saying "I chose Rust because it's fast" — that's a language-fanboy answer. The real 
answer is about correctness boundaries and observability, which is what he cares about. 
T7.  "Where does the data live, and is it encrypted?"
Local SQLite with FTS5 for the ledger and the index, under the app's storage root. Activity metadata, not 
screen recordings and not keystrokes. Today the protection is the OS: FileVault plus the app sandbox 
boundary. Per-record encryption with a key in the Keychain is on the roadmap and is a requirement 
before sync ships — I'm not going to build cross-device sync on an unencrypted local store. 
WARNING
Do not claim end-to-end encryption today. If you do, he will ask which key, where it lives, and what the 
threat model is. "FileVault plus sandbox; per-record encryption before sync" is a credible, honest 
answer.

capture screenshots or audio by default. We store metadata, not content. And every capture surface has 
an explicit pause, per-app exclusion, and a delete-everything control, with no server in the loop. The 
product has to be defensible to a security researcher, not just to a user, because one bad write-up ends a 
local-first company.

T10.  "What does Windows actually cost you?"

16.2  If the answer is yes, what happens next
1. He writes a memo. Expect him to ask for the five documents and two or three customer 
references — which is why you need five real users who will take a call. 
2. Partner meeting (Monday). He presents; you are not in the room. The quality of his memo is the 
quality of your meeting. This is why the one-sentence pitch matters more than any slide. 
3. Diligence: 2–4 weeks. Code review by a technical partner or an outside expert, reference calls, 
market work, sometimes a call with a portfolio founder in the space. 
4. Legal and close: 1–2 weeks. SAFE, cap table, IP assignment, incorporation. If any of these are 
missing, it is the most common cause of a deal slipping a month. 
5. Total: 4–8 weeks on NEA's own typical timeline; faster if the case is uncontested and 
pre-referred. 
WARNING
The three things that kill pre-seed deals between the partner meeting and close: (1) an unassigned IP 
or co-founder equity question, (2) a founder who disappears for a week during diligence, (3) a traction 
number that changes when someone else measures it. All three are preventable this week. 
16.3  Run the process, don't wait for it
One interested associate is not a round. In the same week, write to five more people with the same 
specificity you used with Lila — a piece they wrote, a company they backed, a problem you can tie to 
their thesis. Not a mass email. Five individual emails, each referencing something real. 
GOOD TO KNOW
If Thomas asks "who else are you talking to?", the best answer is the truth told calmly: "Three people 
at funds with a thesis about this. None of them have said yes." That is credible. "We have a lot of 
interest" from a founder with 26 users is not.

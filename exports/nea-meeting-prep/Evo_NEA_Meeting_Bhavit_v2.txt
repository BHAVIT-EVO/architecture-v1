This book exists for one meeting: Friday 23 October 2026, 11:00am Pacific, thirty minutes,
Zoom, with Thomas Joshi, Associate at NEA. Nothing else. It is not a general fundraising guide
and it does not cover any earlier project of yours.
You are a first-time founder and you have two days. So the book is built backwards from a single
question: what does Thomas need to believe at 11:30am on Friday that he does not believe at

The arithmetic that nobody says out loud
Run this once, privately, and then forget the number:
$1.5M into NEA 19 is 0.027% of the fund.
If Evo returned 100× , NEA's $1.5M becomes $150M — about 2.7% of the fund.
To return the whole fund, that single $1.5M cheque would have to become $5.5 billion.
This is why large funds often pass on genuinely good small companies, and it is why the phrase
"too early for us" usually means "too small for the fund." It is not an insult and it is not about you.
But — and this is the part founders miss — it does not kill you. Firms this size routinely write
small cheques for three reasons: to keep a relationship warm, to buy an option on a category, and
because a partner championed it. Your job is to give Thomas a reason that is one of those three,
and to be the obvious choice if the category turns out to be real.
SAY THIS
"I know a $1.5M round is small for NEA 19. I'm not asking you to make it the fund's return. I'm
asking whether the category is one you want a position in before the seed, and whether we're the
right team to hold it."
CAREFUL
Do not open with that. It is a closing argument, not an opening one. It works at 0:25, when he has
already decided you are interesting. Said at 0:05, it is a reason to say no.

TECHNICAL TRUTH
09 What the code actually does — and the
four things it can't
You will not be asked to explain the architecture. You may be asked to demonstrate that you
understand it. This section gives you a plain-English model you can hold in your head, then the
specifics for follow-ups.
The pipeline, in six sentences
1. Capture. A background process reads macOS Accessibility events — which window has focus,
what document it holds, what URL, what path — plus file saves and input activity counts. It never
takes screenshots.
2. Ledger. Those events go into a local append-only SQLite log with full-text search. It is the single
source of truth, and it is deterministic.
3. Episodes. The log is cut into bounded stretches where attention stayed on a stable working set.
Boundaries come from content-tested resumption first, dip absorption second, and the clock only
as fallback.
4. Bodies of work. Episodes are assigned to works. Identity comes from production — what you
typed, saved, downloaded, committed — not from what was merely open. Attention-only resources
become companions and decay.
5. Retrieval. A request — typed or spoken — resolves to one work, or honestly reports ambiguity, or
reports no match. It never silently widens scope.
6. Restoration. Each resource becomes an action: open a URL, open a file, or raise an existing
window if one already shows that exact document. Every attempt lands in a bucket and is
reported.

16 Your questions for him
You get roughly four minutes at the end. Most founders waste them on questions whose answers
are on the website. These eight are chosen because each one either gives you information you
cannot get elsewhere, or makes him say something he'll have to stand behind later.
The three you must ask
1. "What happens next, and on what timeline?"
Ask it every time, at the end, regardless of how the call went. It is not pushy — it is the question a
founder who has done this before asks. Whatever he says, write it down and repeat it back: "So I'll
send the technical write-up Tuesday and we'll speak again the week after — does that sound right?"
Repeating it back converts a vague intention into a commitment.
2. "What would make this a yes for you?"
This is the highest-value question in the meeting. It tells you what to build or prove next, and it
tells you whether he has a specific bar or is just being polite. If the answer is vague, that is
information too.
3. "What's your biggest concern right now?"
Almost nobody asks this and it works almost every time. It gives you the objection while you still
have time to answer it — and if you can answer it well in thirty seconds, you have removed the
thing that would have killed you in the memo.
The three that tell you how to play the next month
4. "How does NEA decide on a round this size, and who's in the room?"
A $1.5M pre-seed at a $38B firm is an unusual shape. You genuinely need to know whether this
goes to partnership, whether an associate can champion a cheque this small, and how long it takes.
Asking directly is not rude; pretending you already know is worse.
5. "Would it help if I sent you the technical write-up, or would you rather dig into
the code yourself?"
Both options flatter him, and his answer tells you how technical his diligence will be. If he wants
the code, you have a real advantage — the repo is genuinely well-documented and the audit
document is honest.
6. "Are there parts of this you'd want me to go deeper on before we speak again?"
This converts the meeting into a homework assignment. It also makes the second call easier to
earn, because you arrive having answered the exact questions he cared about.

7. "What did you think of Computer History when it launched?"
A genuinely interesting question for a person who has written about exactly this. It also invites him
